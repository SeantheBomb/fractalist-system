A Turning is itself a <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/local-rule/">Local Rule</a> — repeated, shared, aimed — which means everything this book has already said about a Local Rule applies to it without exception, including the part most easily forgotten while an occasion is still young: it will, if kept honestly for long enough, stop belonging to whoever first held it.

<a class="concept-link" href="/thirds/the-spark/">The Spark</a> Feast someone throws alone this year may, decades from now, be kept by people who never met them, who have no idea whose practice actually started it, and who are building toward the same <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/global-form/">Global Form</a> regardless. This is not a loss of ownership to mourn. It is <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/becoming-a-third/">Becoming a Third</a>, working exactly as it already promised it would — a pattern outliving the person who first repeated it.

This is the actual, honest answer to the worry this whole Part opened with — that a practice with no history has nothing worth marking. It was never true that a Turning needs a founding event behind it to be worth keeping. It only ever needed a Global Form still worth building ahead of it, and enough people willing to feast, laugh, rest, and toast their way toward it, one honest occasion at a time.

You will not see most of what these occasions eventually build. That was never the part that was supposed to comfort you. The comfort, if there is one, is smaller and sturdier than that: the laying itself was real, whether or not you're there to watch what finally stands on top of it.
