Not every Dyad holds weight equally. A simple diagnostic, borrowed and adapted, gives you a way to actually check whether a Dyad is standing on all three legs it needs:

**Authenticity** — are you actually being yourself, or a managed, performed version of yourself, inside this relationship? A Dyad built on a performance can look stable for a long time before it collapses under the weight of the gap between the performance and the person underneath it.

**Empathy** — can you accurately model what the other person is actually experiencing, distinct from what you'd be experiencing in their position? This is harder than it sounds and easy to fake with sympathy instead.

**Logic** — is there a coherent, honest structure to how you're actually treating each other, or is the relationship running on mood and momentum with no real throughline?

A Dyad missing any one leg tends to compensate, badly, with the other two — heavy on logic and empathy but thin on authenticity produces a relationship that runs smoothly and feels hollow; heavy on authenticity and logic but thin on empathy produces two honest people who keep failing to actually understand each other. **A short practice:** once a month, independently, each person in the Dyad rates the relationship's current strength on all three legs, one to five, then compares notes. The value isn't in the number. It's in discovering where your two independent ratings disagree — that gap is usually where the next honest conversation needs to happen.

**Walked through one real case, the three legs stop being abstract.** Two people, several years in, keep having the same fight about whose career takes priority this year. Checked leg by leg: Authenticity is actually intact — neither is performing contentment they don't feel. Logic is intact too — the actual reasoning behind each position holds up. What's missing is Empathy, specifically: each can state their own case clearly and still can't accurately describe what the *other* person is afraid of losing, only what they're asking for. The fix is not "compromise on the schedule." It is each of them describing the other's fear back, out loud, until it is recognizably right rather than a caricature of it — after which the actual scheduling conversation takes ten minutes.

**One long-studied pattern is worth knowing here, because it explains a specific kind of friction people usually blame on personality mismatch rather than mechanism.** How two people actually behave toward each other, moment to moment, runs on two separate axes that don't work the same way. On warmth — how friendly or cold a person is being — the pattern is correspondence: warmth tends to invite warmth back, coldness tends to invite coldness back, same tone answered with the same tone. On control — who's asserting and who's yielding — the pattern flips entirely: dominance tends to invite deference, and deference tends to invite dominance, opposite answered with opposite, both people settling into a stable, complementary pair without ever discussing it. A relationship where both people keep asserting, neither yielding, isn't always failing at empathy. It can be stuck on the one axis that was never supposed to be met with more of the same.

**And that axis has a consequence this book owes you plainly, because the tables in Part Five are easy to read as assuming something that is often false.** A Dyad-Kin is drawn as two people each running a pattern of comparable weight, meeting as peers. Complementarity says otherwise: in the arrangements built on the control axis, the two people are not running the same rule at all. They are running **fitted opposite** rules, and the pattern is stable precisely because neither half can change without the other's half changing too.

So when you name a Third, there is a second question, and it is the one that decides what you can do: **who is feeding this, and who is absorbing it?** A loop can be entirely reciprocal and not remotely equitable. Two people are in it, and each runs a rule; but one may be setting the terms while the other mostly receives the outcome.

Which gives a case this practice has to say outright. **If you are absorbing and not feeding, it is not your loop to change** — and the honest move is not to hunt harder for your contribution. It is to name what is happening to you as what it is. The Fold chapter already says this as a caution against misusing the idea; it belongs here as a reading, because the misuse it warns against is what a sincere practitioner does by default when the framework only offers them one seat.

**The leverage ladder, walked at this scale, because a Dyad is where it is easiest to aim badly.** The *numbers*-level fix is "we should talk less about money" — a quantity adjustment that never holds. The *buffer* is a bigger emotional reserve — more patience banked before the next argument, still not addressing why the argument keeps recurring. *Information flow* is whether both people can actually see the shared budget rather than one person managing it invisibly. The *rule* is something like "no money conversations after 9pm" — a real structural change, more durable than any of the above. The *goal* underneath the rule is the real fork: is this Dyad organized around "avoid conflict" or around "build something honestly together"? And the *paradigm* — the rarely-examined assumption underneath even that — might be something like "money conversations are inherently dangerous," inherited from somewhere neither person chose, quietly poisoning every lower rung until it's actually named and checked.

**Try this:** think of one recurring friction in a close relationship. Name which axis it actually sits on — warmth or control — before trying to fix it. A warmth problem needs someone to go first with the tone they want back. A control problem needs someone to actually yield, once, on purpose, rather than both people waiting the other out.
