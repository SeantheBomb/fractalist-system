Not every two-person <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/local-rule/">Local Rule</a> holds weight equally. A simple diagnostic, borrowed and adapted, gives you a way to actually check whether a Dyad is standing on all three legs it needs:

**Authenticity** — are you actually being yourself, or a managed, performed version of yourself, inside this relationship? A Dyad built on a performance can look stable for a long time before it collapses under the weight of the gap between the performance and the person underneath it.

**Empathy** — can you accurately model what the other person is actually experiencing, distinct from what you'd be experiencing in their position? This is harder than it sounds and easy to fake with sympathy instead.

**Logic** — is there a coherent, honest structure to how you're actually treating each other, or is the relationship running on mood and momentum with no real throughline?

A Dyad missing any one leg tends to compensate, badly, with the other two — heavy on logic and empathy but thin on authenticity produces a relationship that runs smoothly and feels hollow; heavy on authenticity and logic but thin on empathy produces two honest people who keep failing to actually understand each other. **A short practice:** once a month, independently, each person in the Dyad rates the relationship's current strength on all three legs, one to five, then compares notes. The value isn't in the number. It's in discovering where your two independent ratings disagree — that gap is usually where the next honest conversation needs to happen.

**Walked through one real case, the three legs stop being abstract.** Two people, several years in, keep having the same fight about whose career takes priority this year. Checked leg by leg: Authenticity is actually intact — neither is performing contentment they don't feel. Logic is intact too — the actual reasoning behind each position holds up. What's missing is Empathy, specifically: each can state their own case clearly and still can't accurately describe what the *other* person is afraid of losing, only what they're asking for. The fix was never "compromise on the schedule." It was each of them describing the other's fear back, out loud, until it was recognizably right rather than a caricature of it — after which the actual scheduling conversation took ten minutes.

**One well-documented pattern is worth knowing here, because it explains a specific kind of friction people usually blame on personality mismatch rather than mechanism.** How two people actually behave toward each other, moment to moment, runs on two separate axes that don't work the same way. On warmth — how friendly or cold a person is being — the pattern is correspondence: warmth tends to invite warmth back, coldness tends to invite coldness back, same tone answered with the same tone. On control — who's asserting and who's yielding — the pattern flips entirely: dominance tends to invite deference, and deference tends to invite dominance, opposite answered with opposite, both people settling into a stable, complementary pair without ever discussing it. A relationship where both people keep asserting, neither yielding, isn't failing at empathy. It's stuck on the one axis that was never supposed to be met with more of the same.

**Try this:** think of one recurring friction in a close relationship. Name which axis it actually sits on — warmth or control — before trying to fix it. A warmth problem needs someone to go first with the tone they want back. A control problem needs someone to actually yield, once, on purpose, rather than both people waiting the other out.
