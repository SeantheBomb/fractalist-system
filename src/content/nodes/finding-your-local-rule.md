The Loop pillar, pointed at your own life, asks a specific, practical question: *what are you actually going to repeat?* This chapter is a self-inventory to help answer it — not a personality test, just four honest questions, best answered in writing, that overlap where a sustainable <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/local-rule/">Local Rule</a> tends to live.

**One thing before the questions, because without it this chapter quietly asks you to answer for things that were never yours.** The Nest runs downward as well as up, which means some of the rules you are running right now were not chosen by you. They were installed — by a family, a shortage, a workplace, a feed, a scale above you that never asked. That is arithmetic rather than sympathy: if a <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/global-form/">Global Form</a> at one scale is a Local Rule at the next, going down as surely as going up, then rules arrive from above as a matter of course, and yours did.

So there are two questions here, and they are easy to collapse. *Is this rule mine to work?* and *was this rule my fault?* The first is almost always yes. The second is frequently no, and nothing about the first requires the second. **A Local Rule you did not choose, installed by conditions you did not set, is still yours to work and was never your fault.** Hold both. Dropping the first is helplessness; dropping the second is a self-blame that will make every page after this one harder than it needs to be.

**What do you love enough to repeat without being paid to?**
Not what you're supposed to love. Not what looks good described at a dinner party. What do you actually keep returning to, on your own time, when nothing is making you?

**What are you actually good at — including the parts that feel too easy to count?**
People routinely discount the things that come naturally to them, on the theory that anything that doesn't require struggle can't be a real skill. This is backwards, and worth being direct about: innate aptitude is a starting point, not a destiny, but it's also not nothing. Sustainable growth rarely comes from grinding at your weaknesses until they resemble your strengths. It comes from noticing what already feels easy, and deliberately choosing environments and habits that amplify that edge — treating the rest as context to manage, not a deficit to fix. Talent alone stalls without the harder, less glamorous commitment to actually work within your own nature instead of against it. Look for what's easy for you and hard for most people — that gap is where a real Local Rule tends to live, and it's a more durable foundation than a rule that depends on constant willpower to override what comes naturally.

**What does the situation in front of you actually need — not in the abstract, but from you, specifically, right now?**
This question exists to keep the first two from curdling into self-indulgence. A Local Rule aimed only at your own love and talent, with no contact with anyone else's actual need, tends to stay a hobby instead of becoming a practice with weight to it. It helps here to separate what's being asked for from what's actually needed underneath it — a want is the surface request, but it's usually standing in for a smaller set of more basic needs: to be understood, to belong, to matter, to have some say in your own life. A Local Rule aimed at the surface want burns out fast, because wants shift constantly and a rule chasing them has no stable ground to stand on. A Local Rule aimed at an actual underlying need tends to hold, because needs are far more stable than the wants that express them. This is also where it's worth remembering that need doesn't stop at survival. Alongside the practical ones, people are reaching for competence, for autonomy, and for a sense of genuinely belonging somewhere — not after the practical needs are settled, but at the same time. A Local Rule that answers only the practical layer will feel technically responsible and quietly unsatisfying. Ask not just "what does this situation need from me" but "which layer of need is this actually reaching toward" — the answer changes what a sustainable response looks like.

**What can you sustain — not this month, but for years?**
This is the question people skip, and it's the one that determines whether any of this becomes a <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/third-thing/">Third Thing</a> or just a good week. A Local Rule you love, are good at, and that's needed, but that quietly burns you out, isn't a Local Rule yet. It's a launch. Ask honestly what pace you could keep up if no one was watching and nothing was urgent.

Where these four answers overlap — loved, capable, needed, sustainable — is usually close to the actual Local Rule worth repeating. It rarely arrives as a single clean sentence on the first pass. Write down whatever you've got, even messy, and revisit it in a month. The inventory itself is a loop, not a one-time form.

**This search has a name you already have: <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/elegance/">elegance</a>.** Part One defined it as maximum complexity from minimum complication, and this is that definition applied to a life. A well-designed game doesn't satisfy its separate goals — balance, depth, thematic coherence, replayability — with a separate patched-on rule for each one. It finds the single rule whose interactions already produce all of them at once, as emergent consequences rather than bolted-on exceptions. That's precisely what this four-question search is for. An *inelegant* life, in these terms, is one built from several disconnected Local Rules running in parallel — a job that satisfies "needed," a hobby that satisfies "loved," volunteering that satisfies some other unmet piece, none of them touching each other. An elegant one is a single Local Rule where all four properties fall out of the same repeated act, the personal version of a game mechanic clean enough that its balance and its fun turn out to be the same thing viewed from two angles.

Worth one honest complication before you go chasing pure elegance, though, because the same design discipline that gave us the word also teaches a caution against it: a perfectly smooth, mathematically elegant progression curve is often worse for an actual human than one with deliberate segments and visible breakpoints, because the fast, intuitive part of your mind wants progress it can feel in discrete chunks, not a continuous curve too smooth to register as movement at all. A life pursued toward one seamless, perfectly overlapping Local Rule, with every seam sanded away, risks becoming exactly that kind of curve — clean on paper, flat to actually live inside. Some visible segmentation, some milestone with an actual edge to it, some answers that overlap most of the time rather than perfectly every time, may be doing real motivational work a fully unified life would quietly lose. The target was never elegance for its own sake. It was always the version of elegant that's actually elegant for the specific system running it — and you already know, better than any external framework could tell you, whether you're a mind that thrives on one smooth curve or one that needs the breakpoints to feel like it's moving at all.

One more thing worth saying plainly: this exercise is not about finding your one true purpose, carved into you from birth, waiting to be discovered. That would just be the fixed-self idea wearing a self-help costume. You're not discovering a hidden essence. You're identifying a good candidate rule to start repeating on purpose — one you're allowed to revise the moment it stops being true.

**A check before you commit, and it's a skill worth building on its own: run the candidate rule forward, once per scale, not just once.** The same Local Rule doesn't produce the same Global Form at every size — at each scale it produces whatever has had time to build on top of it, which is a genuinely different thing each time, not just "more of the same, further out." A daily habit of short, clipped replies might look like efficiency this week, at Self scale. Given a year to become raw material for a Dyad, it looks like a slow withdrawal. Given five years to become raw material for a Skein, it looks like a reputation for being hard to reach. The rule never changed. What it compounded into did, at every step someone else's Local Rule had time to build on top of yours.

**Try this:** Take the candidate rule you're considering. Write three short sentences: what this looks like in a week, at your own scale. What it looks like in a year, once it's become part of your closest relationship. What it looks like in five years, once that's become part of how your wider circle knows you. Most people only ever check the first one.

**Before leaving this, one thing about the rule you just named, because it determines whether changing it holds.**

A Local Rule is not the bottom. Underneath any repeated behavior sit two things: a **need** it serves, and a **belief** about how that behavior serves it. *I want to feel like I'm contributing. I think being the one who writes the problem on the board in a meeting is how I do that. So that's what I do.* Behavior is the visible end of a three-link chain, and it is the only link anyone can see — including you, about yourself.

This matters for one practical reason. **A behavior changed without touching the need underneath it is what Chapter 9 will call a Numbers-level intervention, the shallowest kind, and it drifts back** — not because you lacked discipline, but because the need is still there, still unmet, still looking for an output. Whatever you replace it with has to serve that need at least as well, or the old rule returns the moment attention lapses.

So when a rule resists changing, ask downward instead of pushing harder at the top. *What is this actually getting me?* Then: *do I still believe this is how to get it?* The second question is where most of the movement is, because a belief formed by something that worked once tends to outlive the conditions that made it work.
