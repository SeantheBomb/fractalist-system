Four practices carry most of the daily weight in Fractalism. Each is short enough to do inside a normal day, and each is doing something specific and explainable, not just something that feels vaguely spiritual.

### The Local Step / Global Form Breath
*Use this the moment you feel swept into a loop you didn't choose.*

Sit or stand still. On the inhale, think: *this one breath.* On the exhale, think: *the whole of what it builds.* Repeat for ten breaths.

**What it's actually doing:** the spiral you're caught in — anxious, resentful, compulsive — is running on autopilot, the same automatic machinery that makes any well-practiced habit require no conscious thought at all. That machinery is efficient specifically because it doesn't need your attention to start. This practice works by deliberately loading your attention with something specific and present, because a spiral, once running, feeds on attention, and you cannot keep feeding it and consciously track "this one breath, the whole of what it builds" at full capacity at the same time. You're not fighting the spiral head-on. You're quietly occupying the machinery it was running on. A brief, intentional pause like this is itself a trainable rep — the gap between a stimulus and your response to it doesn't widen on its own. It widens because you practiced standing inside it, on purpose, in small moments like this one, until standing there stopped being a struggle and started being a reflex.

### Tree-Gazing
*A grounding practice, best outside, three to five minutes.*

Find any branching thing. Follow one branch to where it splits. Notice: the smaller branch is shaped like the whole tree. Follow it again. Rest your attention on the *repetition*, not the whole shape.

**What it's actually doing:** the training Day 2 of the onboarding began — seeing pattern instead of objects, which is the shift that later lets you notice a loop instead of only its results.

**And the object is not incidental, which is the part that usually reads as arbitrary.** Part Three has a word for the honest, unrolled record of a loop — one root rule branching into a genuinely different leaf every time it meets a new condition — and the word is **Tree**. Sitting with a literal branching thing is direct practice for seeing that: not a metaphor placed alongside the vocabulary but the same shape, at a size you can hold in your eyes, slow enough to study. You are learning to look at something that got where it is by repeating one rule against conditions it did not choose, which is also a description of you.

*A second reason to keep it, for the days you would skip it: directed attention tires with use, and there is evidence that time spent among natural, undemanding things helps restore it. The practice pays most on exactly the day it is hardest to start.*

### Naming the Loop
*Use the moment you catch yourself mid-habit.*

Pause. Silently name the four beats as they're happening: *cue... craving... response... reward.* You don't have to stop the loop. Just naming it while it runs is enough to shift you out of pure autopilot and into awareness — which is the only place a loop can actually be changed from.

**One thing about where in a loop you can actually reach, since the four beats are not equally available.** A cue arrives whether you like it or not. A response is most of the way to automatic before you notice it. A reward is a fact about your nervous system. But the step between cue and craving is different in kind — the craving is produced less by the cue itself than by the *meaning you assign* to the cue. That is the one interpretive beat in an otherwise mechanical sequence, and therefore the one naming touches most directly.

It also tells you what success looks like here, which is easy to practice for months without picturing. It is not the absence of cues. It is noticing one arrive and finding that nothing follows — that you registered the thing and simply did not want to change anything about where you were. There is no drama in that state at all, which is why most people walk straight past it.

**What it's actually doing:** the fast, automatic part of your mind and the slower, deliberate part of your mind trade off control based on how much attention a task demands. Naming something forces the slower system online, even briefly. It's a small tax on the automatic loop, paid in exactly the currency — attention — that automatic loops are designed to avoid spending.

### Seeding
*A standing practice for how you speak, to yourself and others.*

Before a hard conversation, or when you catch a harsh thought about yourself, pause and ask: *is this a seed of fear, or a seed of care?* Not "is it true or false" — seeds aren't judged that way. Ask what it will grow into if you keep planting it.

A simple version for the morning: *"I tend the field of my mind today. Let what I repeat be worth becoming."*
A simple version for closing the day: *"What I did today becomes what I do tomorrow. Let today's small step be worth repeating."*

Residue collects at the edges of a day the way fluid pools at the body's extremities — neither one returns to the center on its own. That's the first case for the evening version. You don't need a catastrophe to justify it.

**What it's actually doing:** every thought you repeat gets easier to think again — that's simply what repetition does to a mental pathway, the same mechanism that turns a difficult new skill into an effortless one. That mechanism doesn't check whether what it's making effortless is good for you. Seeding is choosing, on purpose, what you're willing to make effortless.

**This practice is easy to misread in one way, and the misreading changes what it asks of you.** Seeding is not something you start doing. You are planting either way — every repeated thought, every sentence said in a particular tone, every small thing done again. Nothing here begins the planting. The only thing the practice changes is whether you can see what is going into the ground, and seeing it is what makes a choice available that was not available before.

**And it has a second beat the other practices lack: go back and look.** Before the day ends, check one thing you planted. Not to judge the crop — most of what you plant today will show nothing today. To confirm the ground answers at all. That is the fast return, and it is what keeps the sense of having any hand in your own life fed while the slow things are still underground.

Its failure mode is already written into one of Part Five's figures: what you attend to grows, and so does whatever you keep confirming. Go looking for returns and you will find the ones you went looking for. The correction is the ordinary one — look for what actually happened, not for the evidence you wanted.

**These four practices carry the four moves between them, which is worth saying once so they stop looking like a list.** Tree-Gazing and Naming the <a class="concept-link" href="/concept/loop/">Loop</a> are the same skill aimed two ways — pattern outward, pattern inward. Seeding is what goes in. The breath is thinning: pulling out what has started to take over. And the second beat above, going back to what you planted, is where tending starts. Look, plant, tend, thin. That is the whole of it, and everything else in this part is those four moves at a larger scale or with another person in the room.
